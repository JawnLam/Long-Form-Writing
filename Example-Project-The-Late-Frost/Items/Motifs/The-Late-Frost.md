# Motif — The Late Frost

## What this motif is

The novel's title image and primary external pressure. The frost is literally the weather event that threatens the harvest; symbolically it is the late-arriving cold that exposes what has been pretending to be safe. The frost is the novel's emotional engine, its plot engine, and its image-cluster.

## Why this motif

- It is the external clock for the three-week timeframe
- It is the work the sisters must do together regardless of the relationship's state — the labor of frost-defense is the floor under everything else
- It is the central image: late frost is *unseasonable* — a cold that arrives after spring has already begun. The novel is about late cold in the sisters' relationship and in the family's financial life: the cold that arrives after warmth had been assumed

## Image-cluster vocabulary (for prose consistency)

- *Late* (as adjective and as accusation)
- *Bud-break*, *blackened*, *browned*, *blistered* (the sensory vocabulary of frost damage)
- *Wind machine*, *smudge pot*, *propane* (the equipment vocabulary)
- *28 degrees*, *three hours* (the technical specificity)
- *Sonoma Mountains*, *fog burning off*, *valley floor* (the geographical vocabulary)
- Avoid: *Jack Frost*, *frosty* (twee diction), any wine-country marketing-copy vocabulary

## What it must NOT do

- The motif must not become explicit symbolism. No character ever notices that the literal frost is also a metaphoric one. The prose does the work; the characters live inside it.
- The motif must not be flattened into a one-line resonance. The frost is itself, first. The metaphoric layer is allowed to accumulate only by repeated literal presence.
