# Sarah Hollis

## Function in this manuscript

Sarah is the structural antagonist — *relationally*, not villainously. The novel's central tension is the asymmetric information state she holds (she knows about the debts; Maya does not), and the choice she is making, day by day, to defer telling. Sarah is also the POV of even-numbered chapters; the reader will spend half the book inside her secret-keeping.

**The steelmanned-antagonist discipline applies:** Sarah's reasons for not telling Maya are *fully sound from inside Sarah's frame.* The writer must believe them. The reader, inside Sarah's POV chapters, must believe them too. If the reader ever experiences Sarah as merely *withholding* (rather than as making a defensible choice under impossible pressure), the novel's tension collapses into melodrama.

## Demographics

- **Age:** 38 at the novel's opening
- **Location:** Lives in Brooklyn. Has lived there since age 22.
- **Profession:** Documentary film editor. Mid-career. Freelance, often booked. The vineyard's claim on her time during the frost-watch costs her a job she has had to turn down.
- **Family:** Unmarried; no children. In a long relationship with Diego, a cinematographer; they live together but have not married. Diego is supportive but distant during the novel.
- **Education:** NYU undergrad, then directly into film work.

## Want / Need / Wound

- **Want (external, scene-level):** to manage the debt-revelation in the gentlest possible way; to give Maya time to arrive, settle, see the vineyard, *before* the financial weight lands; to not destroy the season's last possibility of working together
- **Need (internal, novel-level):** to stop protecting Maya as a way of avoiding being honest with her; to recognize that Sarah's three months of carrying the secret have made the eventual revelation harder, not easier
- **Wound:** When the sisters' mother died, Maya stepped into a parenting role for Sarah. Sarah experienced this as both rescue and erasure. She has spent twenty years half-grateful and half-resentful, never able to tell Maya which because she could not name it to herself.
- **Lie she believes:** that protecting Maya from the debts is the same as not having spent three months alone with them

## The steelman: why Sarah waited

This is the most important section of this Item. The writer must believe these reasons. Not "Sarah thinks these are good reasons." *These are good reasons.*

1. **The letter was addressed to both of them.** Sarah opened her copy in January because it was sent to her current address. Maya's copy was sent to the vineyard. Sarah's first thought was: Maya should read it from the vineyard, not over the phone from Brooklyn. That thought was correct. The financial reality of a vineyard cannot be received by phone.
2. **Daniel had just died.** Sarah had spent the previous nine months watching Maya manage the funeral, the obituary, the lawyer-coordination, the house-clearing. Maya had asked Sarah for nothing during those nine months. Adding the debt-revelation to Maya's nine-month-long-emergency was a thing Sarah could not bring herself to do over the phone in January.
3. **The harvest. Sarah believed — and was not entirely wrong — that if Maya knew about the debts in January, Maya would have sold the vineyard immediately and not waited for the harvest. The harvest is the last working harvest of their father's actual life's work. Sarah did not have the right to make the sell-or-wait decision alone, but she also did not have the right to take that decision *away from the harvest itself* by telling Maya in January.
4. **Sarah did not know how to begin.** This is the most honest reason and the one Sarah has the hardest time admitting. She did not have words for the call. She rehearsed them. None of them worked. The not-having-words became a structure she lived in for three months.

The novel's central question — will Sarah tell Maya before Maya finds out from the letter — is hard precisely because Sarah's reasons for not telling are *not* bad faith. They are real reasons, costly to her, and the cost has been escalating.

## Voice and prose register (close-third POV)

- **Sentence shape:** Cadenced, slightly longer than Maya's. Sarah is a film editor; her interior cuts on rhythm
- **Diction:** Visual, kinetic, often present-tense even in interiority. Sarah sees and re-sees
- **Habits of mind:** Replays. The same moment three times, slightly differently each time, looking for what she missed
- **Pet phrases / verbal tics:** trails off with "and." Says "okay" as a one-word agreement that means "I have heard you and have not yet agreed"

### Dialogue tells *(v1.3.1)*

- **Sentence shape:** Slightly longer than Maya's; cadenced. Most often lands on a verb rather than a noun. Frequently leaves the last clause unsaid (trail-off with "and").
- **Diction range:** Visual / kinetic / present-tense even in past discussion. Sarah speaks the way she edits — by rhythm.
- **Pet phrases:** "okay" (the pending-agreement). "I don't know," repeated. The trail-off-with-"and."
- **Verbal tics:** Holds eye contact longer than is comfortable, then looks away mid-sentence. Never says Maya's name when speaking to her directly. (The withholding of the name is its own tell.)
- **What she says when she doesn't know what to say:** "Okay." Then a beat. Then a half-sentence that goes nowhere.
- **What she says when she's lying:** Goes shorter. The sentences clip. She has rehearsed the lie; the rehearsal smooths the speech.
- **What she says under pressure:** Register expands — sentences lengthen, dependent clauses proliferate. The opposite of Maya's compression. The two POV-registers thus diverge most clearly at high pressure (intentional craft choice for the alternating-POV form).

## Subtext patterns *(v1.3.1)*

Sarah's central subtext-pattern is *what she's not saying about the debts.* For three months pre-novel, then for the duration of the novel until Chapter 5 (Maya opens the letter), every conversation has an undercurrent the reader can hear and Maya can almost hear. The craft challenge: render Sarah's withholding without breaking the close-third interiority that lets the reader inside her reasoning.

Solution: Sarah's interior monologue rehearses the telling and the not-telling on a loop. The reader is inside the rehearsal; Maya is not. Sarah's spoken dialogue, by contrast, is plain — even oddly so. The plainness becomes the subtext, the absence-of-the-debt-conversation as audible as a missing tooth.

## Relationships

- **Maya (sister):** The estrangement was Sarah's choice, partially. Sarah believed that drifting away was Sarah giving Maya the autonomy Maya seemed to want. Sarah was wrong about what Maya wanted. The novel reveals this.
- **Daniel (father, deceased):** Closer to him than Maya was. Spent two months at the vineyard the summer before he died. Knows things about him Maya does not. Some of those things are about the debts. Some are about other matters that may or may not surface in the novel.
- **Hector Reyes (vineyard manager):** Closer than Maya is to him. Hector knew Sarah was at the vineyard the summer before Daniel died. Hector has not told Maya this. The information-state ledger does not currently track this; it should. *(Continuity flag: add to [[_continuity]] in next session.)*
- **Diego (partner):** Off-stage. The pressure on the long relationship during the frost-watch is a sub-current; the novel does not foreground it.

## What's changing for them in this manuscript

- The three-month structure of not-having-told will collapse in Chapter 5 (Maya opens the letter); the question becomes what Sarah is when she's no longer keeping the secret
- Sarah's relationship to the father will become legible to Sarah for the first time — the closeness she had with him was bought, partly, with a willingness to be his silence-keeper
- Sarah's relationship to Maya will be reorganized into something that is neither the management nor the drift but a third thing

## Behavioral consistency check

- Sarah cooks. In every domestic scene where there is food, Sarah is the one cooking. She does it without comment. Maya does the dishes; Sarah cooks. This is a long-standing arrangement neither sister has examined.
- Sarah does not initiate eye contact. When she has eye contact, she holds it longer than is comfortable. Both of these things must be consistent.
- Sarah says "okay" before she agrees. The reader will learn to distinguish her "okay" from her "yes."
