# <Manuscript Title> — Freytag's Pyramid Overlay

> **Gustav Freytag's five-act analysis of dramatic structure (1863). Best fit: classical tragedy, dramatic structure, literary fiction whose shape is broadly classical. The oldest overlay shipped; often the most useful for literary work because it is least prescriptive.**

## The five beats

| # | Beat | Question | Scene(s) | Notes |
|---|------|----------|----------|-------|
| 1 | **Exposition** | What is the world? Who is the protagonist? What's the inciting condition? | | |
| 2 | **Rising Action** | What complications escalate the situation? | | |
| 3 | **Climax** | What's the turning point? *(For Freytag the climax is the MIDDLE, not the end — the moment after which the protagonist's fortunes are committed to a direction)* | | |
| 4 | **Falling Action** | What unfolds from the climax's commitment? | | |
| 5 | **Catastrophe / Dénouement** | What's the final state? *(Freytag's original term is "catastrophe" — tragic outcome; modern usage softens to "dénouement" for non-tragic outcomes)* | | |

## Shape-fit assessment

The Freytag pyramid is **rising-then-falling** — the climax is at the apex, the falling action and dénouement complete the arc on the other side. This is structurally different from Save the Cat or Story Circle, both of which place the climax near the *end*. For literary fiction that lives in the dénouement, Freytag fits.

## Divergence notes

*Most modern fiction places climax near the end and uses a short dénouement; pure Freytag (climax in the middle, substantial falling action) is rarer. If the manuscript follows the modern pattern, Save the Cat or Story Circle may fit better. If the manuscript lingers in the aftermath of the climax, Freytag is the right lens.*

## Risks

- **Tragic-Catastrophe expectation.** Freytag described tragedy; the catastrophe beat is the tragic outcome. For non-tragic stories, the dénouement substitutes — but the *shape* still expects a substantial post-climax movement, which not all modern fiction wants.
- **Climax-in-the-middle confusion.** If the climax is placed at 75% (Save the Cat's "All Is Lost") instead of 50%, the manuscript is not following Freytag — it's following the modern arc. The overlay may be the wrong lens.

## How to use this overlay

- During OUTLINE on a literary or classical cartridge: check whether the dénouement is doing structural work or is afterthought
- During READ-THROUGH: identify whether the climax-as-turning-point lands where Freytag expects, and whether the falling action earns its space
- During REVISE: a saggy second-half of a literary novel often is a falling-action that isn't earning its weight; Freytag is the diagnostic lens
