# The Persistence Question — Outline (v3)

## Book-level shape

Argumentative trade non-fiction. The argument unfolds across eight chapters:

- **Chapters 1–2:** Set up the question and the four-conditions framework, then apply it to one detailed institutional case (Antarctic Treaty system).
- **Chapters 3–5:** Apply the framework to three more cases of different shapes — family business (Hoshi Ryokan), religious order (Benedictine monasticism), constitutional republic (United States).
- **Chapter 6:** Counter-examples. Things that *should* have persisted by the framework and didn't, or that *shouldn't* have and did. The honest argumentation work.
- **Chapter 7:** Integration. What the four cases together show. The limits of the framework.
- **Chapter 8:** Conclusion. What persistent things have in common we already knew; what we now know we didn't before; what this means for institutions being designed today.

Total target: 70,000–80,000 words. Trade non-fiction pacing — each chapter ~7,500–10,000 words.

## Parts

*Not using formal "Parts." The eight chapters compose the book directly.*

## Chapters

### Chapter 1: The Question

- **Purpose:** Open the book. Pose the central question. Introduce the four-conditions framework. State the project's scope and method.
- **Scope:** Set up; not yet applying the framework to a case. ~7,500 words.
- **Sections:**
  1. [[01-01-The-Question]] — opening; the question and why it matters
  2. [[01-02-Four-Conditions-Introduced]] — the framework, briefly
  3. [[01-03-Methodology-Note]] — what counts as evidence; what doesn't; the book's epistemic stance
- **Threads engaged:** All four (distributed legitimacy, adaptive ritual, selection pressure, written tradition)
- **Status:** drafted; awaits structural revision once Chapters 2–3 are drafted

### Chapter 2: The Antarctic Treaty System

- **Purpose:** First detailed worked example. The Antarctic Treaty (1959) is the most institutionally elegant case the writer has — explicit, multi-stakeholder, designed-from-scratch, sixty-five years old, still functional. Use it to make the framework concrete.
- **Scope:** Treaty history → current functioning → analysis against the four conditions → what makes it persist where similar arrangements (League of Nations) didn't. ~9,000 words.
- **Sections:**
  1. [[02-01-Antarctic-Opening-Scene]] — opening narrative; researchers at McMurdo
  2. [[02-02-1959-Treaty-Conditions]] — the political/strategic context that produced the treaty
  3. [[02-03-The-Treaty-Today]] — current functioning
  4. [[02-04-Treaty-Analyzed-Against-Conditions]] — applying the framework
- **Threads engaged:** Distributed-Legitimacy (strong), Adaptive-Ritual (medium), Selection-Pressure (medium — the treaty has survived genuine challenges), Written-Tradition (medium)
- **Status:** drafting; over target word count; structural pass scheduled

### Chapter 3: Family-Business Persistence — The Hoshi Ryokan Case

- **Purpose:** Second worked example. Different shape — a family firm, not an institution. Hoshi Ryokan (Komatsu, Japan) has operated in the same family since 718 AD. Use it to extend the framework into a non-state, non-formal-institution domain.
- **Scope:** Hoshi as case → statistical framing of multi-century family firms → conditions applied. ~9,000 words.
- **Sections:**
  1. [[03-01-Hoshi-Opening]] — opening narrative scene at the ryokan
  2. [[03-02-Family-Business-Statistics]] — how rare is this? statistical framing
  3. [[03-03-Four-Conditions-As-Applied-To-Families]] — how the framework adapts for family firms
  4. [[03-04-Hoshi-Through-The-Framework]] — the case analyzed
  5. [[03-05-Other-Long-Lived-Family-Firms]] — Kongo Gumi (Japanese construction, 578–2006), Stiftskeller St. Peter (Austrian restaurant, 803), Antinori (Italian wine, 1385), with shorter treatment
- **Threads engaged:** All four
- **Status:** Section 01 drafting; rest planned

### Chapter 4: Religious Orders — Benedictine Monasticism

- **Purpose:** Third case. Religious institution operating since 529 AD with explicit textual codification of practice (Rule of St. Benedict). Use to show how *explicit codification* of practice contributes to persistence — but also how the order's adaptive flexibility within the Rule is what makes it work.
- **Scope:** Founding context → the Rule → contemporary practice → conditions applied. ~9,000 words.
- **Sections (planned):**
  1. Opening narrative — a contemporary monastery's daily office
  2. The Rule and its function — codified practice that flexes
  3. 1,500 years of adaptation within the form
  4. Conditions applied to monastic orders
  5. Comparison to orders that didn't persist (e.g., Carthusians at smaller scale; failed reform movements)
- **Threads engaged:** Written-Tradition (strong), Adaptive-Ritual (strong), Distributed-Legitimacy (medium — through obedience to Rule, not to any specific abbot), Selection-Pressure (medium)
- **Status:** planned

### Chapter 5: Constitutional Republics — The United States Case

- **Purpose:** Fourth case. A modern republic in its 235th year. Use to test the framework on a familiar, contested case. Surface where the framework predicts persistence and where it predicts vulnerability.
- **Scope:** Constitutional founding → the working system → analysis. ~9,000 words.
- **Sections (planned):**
  1. The founding moment as institutional design
  2. The Constitution's distinctive features against the four conditions
  3. Adaptive ritual through amendment and judicial interpretation
  4. Selection pressure — what has stressed the system and how it absorbed the stress
  5. Current vulnerabilities through the framework's lens
- **Threads engaged:** All four
- **Note:** This is the politically-sensitive chapter. Voice-mode care; counter-arguments surfaced honestly.
- **Status:** planned

### Chapter 6: Counter-Examples

- **Purpose:** The honest argumentation chapter. Cases that shouldn't have persisted by the framework but did, and cases that should have but didn't.
- **Scope:** Three or four short studies. ~8,000 words.
- **Sections (planned):**
  1. North Korea — a regime that shouldn't persist by Acemoglu-Robinson's framework but has. What does the four-conditions framework say?
  2. The Roman Republic 509–27 BC — should have persisted; didn't. What was missing?
  3. Medieval Italian city-republics — mixed-constitution attempts (Venice persisted ~1100 years; Florence ~350; many others <200). What separated them?
  4. The Soviet Union — 1922–1991. Why did it persist as long as it did? Why did it fail when it did?
- **Threads engaged:** All four, in tension
- **Status:** planned

### Chapter 7: Integration

- **Purpose:** What do the four positive cases + counter-examples together show? Pattern across cases. Limits of the framework.
- **Scope:** Synthesis. ~7,000 words.
- **Sections (planned):**
  1. Conditions in combination — not independent variables
  2. Where the framework is strongest
  3. Where the framework is weakest
  4. What we now know that we didn't
- **Threads engaged:** All four — the synthesis
- **Status:** planned

### Chapter 8: Conclusion

- **Purpose:** What does this mean for institutions being designed today? Light touch — not prescriptive policy. Suggestive.
- **Scope:** ~5,000 words.
- **Sections (planned):**

## Structural decisions logged

### 2026-04-15 — Decision: Argument-driven, not chronological

**Decided:** Organize by case-study chapters, not by chronology. Each chapter is a worked example with the framework applied.

**Alternatives considered:**
- Pure chronological (ancient → modern persistence cases): rejected — collapses the framework's structural insight under a timeline narrative
- One enormous case study + theory: rejected — single case can't bear an argument this broad

**Rationale:** The book is about the framework, not about any single case. Four cases let the framework be tested across very different domains (treaty, family, religion, state).

---

### 2026-04-22 — Decision: Four conditions, not three or five

**Decided:** Four conditions — distributed legitimacy, adaptive ritual, selection pressure that's hostile but not lethal, written tradition for reconstruction.

**Alternatives considered:**
- Three conditions (collapsed adaptive ritual into distributed legitimacy): rejected — these are genuinely separable; many institutions have one without the other
- Five conditions (added "shared identity"): rejected — shared identity is downstream of the other four, not independent

**Rationale:** Four is the sweet spot — enough to do real explanatory work; few enough to remember and apply.

---

### 2026-05-10 — Decision: Lead with Antarctic Treaty, not Roman Empire

**Decided:** Chapter 2 (first detailed case) is the Antarctic Treaty.

**Alternatives considered:**
- Lead with Rome: rejected — too over-determined; readers come with too many priors
- Lead with Hoshi Ryokan: rejected — small-scale; doesn't establish the institutional stakes

**Rationale:** Antarctic Treaty is sixty-five years old, designed-from-scratch, multi-stakeholder, geopolitically real, low-emotional-load for most readers. Lets the framework do explanatory work without fighting reader's priors. The familiar cases (Rome, US Constitution) come later when the framework is established.
