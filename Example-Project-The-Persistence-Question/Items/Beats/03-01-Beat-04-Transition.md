# Beat 4: Transition — Setting Up the Statistical Question

## Purpose

Close Section 3.1 by setting up the question Section 3.2 will answer: *how rare is this, really?* The beat needs to:

1. Acknowledge the reader's natural skeptical response (after Beats 1–3, the reader has been given a vivid case but may be unsure whether to trust the dramatic framing)
2. Pose the statistical question explicitly
3. Set up Section 3.2 without giving away its answer

The beat is intentionally short — likely 150–250 words. Its job is structural transition, not content delivery.

The beat should *shift register* — from the sensory present-tense of Beats 1–3 into the analytical register of Section 3.2. This shift is delicate; voice samples confirm the writer can do this kind of register shift cleanly but it requires care.

## Content

*To be drafted.*

Approach options:

**Option A (recommended):** Acknowledge the natural skepticism. *"The natural question, after reading three paragraphs that frame a small Japanese inn as remarkable, is whether the framing is doing work or merely being dramatic. The question is fair. Hoshi Ryokan is presented in this book as a particularly clean case of family-business persistence, but the persistence itself is not a freak outlier — it sits within a small but real population of multi-century family firms whose statistical existence is itself the next question worth examining."*

**Option B:** Move directly to the question. *"How rare is this? Some answers are possible."*

**Option C:** Use a comparative anchor. *"For context: the average lifespan of a Fortune 500 company is roughly twenty years. Hoshi has been operating since before paper money existed."*

**Decision needed:** A vs. B vs. C, or a hybrid. The voice samples favor A — the acknowledgment of skepticism is the writer's natural move when shifting register. Recommend A.
