# The Domain Expert

## Who they are

A specialist in one of the case domains the book treats — a classical historian for the Roman material, a Benedictine monastic scholar for Chapter 4, a constitutional scholar for Chapter 5, a Japan historian for the Hoshi Ryokan material. Different chapters have different Domain Experts; this Reader Item collapses them into one composite figure who represents the cumulative scrutiny the book will face from specialists.

The book is a trade non-fiction synthesis — not a specialist monograph — so this reader is not the primary audience. But they read it, and if the book's treatment of their specific field crosses from "appropriately simplified" into "wrong," they say so publicly, and that damages the book's credibility for everyone else.

## Background they bring

- Deep familiarity with the literature of their specific area
- Sensitivity to oversimplification that crosses into error
- A working knowledge of the methodological disputes within their field
- Strong opinions about what "the consensus" is and where the consensus is actually contested
- The reflex to fact-check specific dates, names, and claims that fall within their expertise

## What they reward

- Honest acknowledgment that the book is a synthesis, not a specialist treatment
- Citations to the strong recent scholarship in their field (not just the canonical 30-year-old texts)
- Distinctions that are simplified for trade audience but flagged as simplified
- Care with terminology — using "the Republic" carefully if writing about Rome; using "the Order" carefully if writing about Benedictines
- Engagement with the methodological debates in their field, even briefly

## What they punish

- Oversimplification that crosses into wrong
- Treating contested findings as settled
- Treating canonical-but-superseded scholarship as authoritative (citing only Gibbon for Roman decline, ignoring the Heather/Ward-Perkins debate)
- Romanticization of the case being studied (the Antarctic Treaty as utopian; the Hoshi Ryokan as mystical Japanese persistence)
- Failure to engage the obvious counter-cases within the domain
- Dates wrong. Names misspelled. Periods conflated.

## Where they resist

- **Specific factual claims in their domain.** Every date, every name, every characterization of a debate. If the writer says "the Antarctic Treaty was negotiated in 1957," this reader catches it.
- **Generalizations across cultures.** The Domain Expert in Japanese studies will resist generalizations from Hoshi Ryokan to "Japanese culture"; the Roman historian will resist generalizations from the Roman Republic to "ancient mixed constitutions."
- **The contestability of "consensus."** Where the book treats something as settled scholarship that's actually contested within the field, this reader pushes back.

## What they're patient with vs. impatient with

- **Patient with:** appropriately scoped synthesis; explicit acknowledgments of where the simplification is happening; citations that show the writer has actually read the recent literature
- **Impatient with:** trade-non-fiction overconfidence; reliance on older or popularized scholarship when better recent work exists; specific factual errors

## Notes

This reader doesn't need to be flattered, but they need to be respected. The book's relationship with this reader is the question: do they finish the chapter on their field thinking "this writer did their homework," or do they finish it thinking "I have to write a review correcting the record"?

For READER-SIMULATION sessions in a specific chapter, treat the Domain Expert as specialized to that chapter's field. The Roman chapter's Domain Expert is a different person than the Benedictine chapter's Domain Expert; both are subsumed in this composite Item, but a READER-SIMULATION should foreground whichever expertise is relevant to the section being read.

For the AI: the most useful resistance from this reader is specific. "The Domain Expert will dispute that 'the Antarctic Treaty was the first international agreement of its kind' — there are several similar pre-1959 arrangements" is useful. "The Domain Expert may not love the simplifications" is not.
