# The Impatient Generalist

## Who they are

Smart, busy, curious adult reader. Mid-career professional in a field unrelated to institutional history or political economy. Reads ten to twenty non-fiction books a year; abandons roughly one in three. Subscribes to The Atlantic, listens to Ezra Klein or Sam Harris or similar in podcast form, reads The Economist for headlines but rarely articles in depth. Picks up a book like this because the premise was interesting at the bookstore and abandons it by page 60 if the payoff isn't visible.

This reader is the book's commercial test. The Skeptic decides whether the argument holds; this reader decides whether the writer can hold an intelligent reader's attention long enough for the argument to land.

## Background they bring

- General intellectual literacy at the educated-magazine-reader level
- Knows the names "Rome," "Antarctic Treaty," "Mary Beard," "Acemoglu" but doesn't necessarily know what each was about specifically
- Comfortable with structured argument when the structure is signposted; lost when the structure is implicit
- No specific historical-period expertise
- Patient with high-quality narrative; impatient with academic prose-style

## What they reward

- Concrete opening scenes that anchor abstract argument (lead with the example, then the principle)
- Clear signposting — at the top of long sections, "here's what this section will show"; at the end, "here's what we just saw and where we're going"
- Dry humor, sparingly used, that doesn't undercut seriousness
- Specific, vivid details that make institutions and historical episodes feel real (the kind of detail that makes a 718 AD inn feel like a place, not a footnote)
- Strong chapter endings that land the chapter's claim and set up the next
- A sense that the writer has thought about *this reader*, not just the academic peers

## What they punish

- Long stretches without visible payoff (the abandoned-at-page-60 risk)
- Pacing that's slow because every alternative was thoroughly engaged before the main argument advanced
- Footnote density that makes the prose feel academic
- Section titles that don't telegraph what the section is doing
- Argumentative throat-clearing — three paragraphs of "let me first establish that..."
- Drifting between abstract argument and concrete case without surfacing the transition

## Where they resist

- **Methodology chapter.** This reader will skim the methodology note, fast. If it's longer than ~2,000 words this reader has already moved on.
- **Pure-argument chapters.** Chapter 7 (Integration) and Chapter 8 (Conclusion) are at highest risk. If they don't have concrete grounding in cases, this reader skips them.
- **Footnote-dense passages.** When the footnotes get thick, the eye glazes. This reader doesn't read footnotes unless surprised by something.
- **Sections that exist for completeness.** If a section feels like it's there because the author thought "I should cover X" rather than because the argument needed X, this reader notices the energy drop.

## What they're patient with vs. impatient with

- **Patient with:** vivid narrative; specific cases; honest engagement with counterargument when it's brief; a writer who is clearly trying to land the argument rather than dump knowledge
- **Impatient with:** academic posturing; hedging stacks; long sentences with multiple subordinate clauses; sections that don't end with a payoff; chapter openings that don't make clear what the chapter is for; footnotes longer than two lines

## Notes

This reader is who decides whether the book sells. The Skeptic is whether the book holds up. Both matter. A book that satisfies only the Skeptic gets a 4-star Amazon review from 12 academic readers and disappears. A book that satisfies only the Generalist gets the airport-bookstore spread and embarrasses the author in academic settings. The book is for both.

For READER-SIMULATION sessions: when reading as the Impatient Generalist, track engagement-loss moments (where the reader starts skimming) and clarity-resistance moments (where the reader can't tell what's being argued or why this matters). Less about whether the argument holds and more about whether it lands.
