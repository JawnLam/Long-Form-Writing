# Hoshi Ryokan Family Records (collected primary sources)

## Citation

*Hoshi Ryokan Family Records*. Komatsu, Ishikawa Prefecture, Japan. (Family archive; access by arrangement with the operating generation. Primary-source collection comprising registry documents, tax records, succession notes, and chronicle entries from 718 to the present.)

For the manuscript: cite as a collective primary-source corpus. Specific document citations to be developed during the Section 3.4 drafting and the Chapter 3 fact-checking pass.

## Why it matters here

Hoshi Ryokan is the central case of Chapter 3. The family-archive records are the primary-source backbone of:

- The 718 AD founding-year claim
- The 46-generation continuity claim
- The Taichō dream-vision founding narrative
- The post-1872 rebuilding details (the current building dates from the 1872 fire)
- Tax records demonstrating continuous business operation across the Tokugawa, Meiji, Taishō, Shōwa, Heisei, and Reiwa periods
- Succession patterns within the family across generations

Without access to these records, the chapter would rely on secondary literature and tourist material, which is methodologically weaker. The Source Item is marked `identified` because the writer has not yet been to Komatsu to engage the archive directly; remote access via correspondence has begun but is incomplete.

## Worries

- **Access is incomplete.** Marked `lfw_status: identified` rather than `ingested`. The writer needs to either visit the archive directly or work through formal correspondence with the family to access primary materials. Drafting in Sections 3.4 and beyond will need to wait on this.
- **Family-curated source.** The records have been preserved and curated by the family; they reflect what the family has chosen to preserve. Independent verification (Japanese government records, prefectural histories, scholarly literature on the inn) is needed for high-stakes claims (especially the 718 AD date).
- **Translation risk.** The records are in classical Japanese, modern Japanese, and historical script forms across the centuries. The writer does not read classical Japanese; translation is necessary for direct citation. This is a real research-budget consideration for the project.
- **Romanization conventions.** "Hoshi" — the standard Romanization used in most Western sources. Japanese-language sources may use other transliterations. Lock the convention before drafting prose to avoid inconsistency.
