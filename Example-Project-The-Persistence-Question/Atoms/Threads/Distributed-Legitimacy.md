# Distributed Legitimacy

## What this thread is

The first of the book's four structural conditions for persistence. Persistent institutions distribute legitimacy across multiple holders rather than concentrating it in any single person, office, or external authority. No single point of failure means no single point at which the institution can be killed by removing one thing.

In the book's argument, "distributed legitimacy" is a specific technical sense — not the political-theory sense of *democratic* legitimacy, but the structural sense of *how many independent supports* an institution rests on.

A consulting firm with a charismatic founder has *concentrated* legitimacy: when the founder retires or dies, the firm typically does not survive the transition. A consulting firm built around a partnership structure with shared decision rights has *distributed* legitimacy: any single partner can leave without the firm collapsing.

The thread is one of the book's load-bearing arguments. It runs through every case study chapter.

## Sources that support it

- [[Acemoglu-Robinson-Why-Nations-Fail-2012]] — the "inclusive vs. extractive institutions" framework is largely about legitimacy distribution at the political level. Relevant but framed differently.
- [[Beard-SPQR-2015]] — the Roman Republic's collegiality (two consuls, plebeian tribunes, mixed-constitution dynamics) was a paradigmatic distributed-legitimacy structure. Its breakdown corresponds to the Republic's failure.
- [[Polybius-Histories-150-BCE]] — primary-source articulation of mixed-constitution thinking; "distributed legitimacy" in this thread is partly a modernization of Polybius's argument.
- [[Hoshi-Ryokan-Records]] — family-business documentation of multi-generational legitimacy transfer

## Sources that complicate or contradict it

- [[Mancur-Olson-Power-and-Prosperity-2000]] — the "stationary bandit" model suggests that some concentrated legitimacy structures (effective autocracies) can produce institutional stability over multi-decade periods. Possible counter-example case (Singapore? Imperial China at various periods?) for Chapter 6.
- [[Francis-Fukuyama-Origins-2011]] — argues that the historical sequence matters more than the legitimacy-distribution shape; some societies achieved persistence through concentrated legitimacy plus state capacity. Engage in Chapter 7 (the framework's limits).
- *Counter-case to surface in Chapter 6:* North Korea — a regime that lacks distributed legitimacy almost completely and has nonetheless persisted for 75+ years. The framework needs to engage why.

## Arc across the book

- **Chapter 1:** Introduced as one of the four conditions, briefly.
- **Chapter 2:** Developed in depth using the Antarctic Treaty. Most paradigmatic case.
- **Chapter 3:** Adapted to the family-firm domain — legitimacy distributed across *generations* rather than across persons in a single moment.
- **Chapter 4:** Adapted to monastic order — legitimacy distributed across the Rule (codified text) plus the local abbot's discretion, rather than concentrated in any pope or bishop.
- **Chapter 5:** Adapted to constitutional republic — the most familiar form to the reader; legitimacy distributed across branches, levels, and parties.
- **Chapter 6:** Surfaces honest complications — cases where the condition's absence didn't kill the institution, and cases where the condition's presence didn't save it.
- **Chapter 7:** Synthesized with the other three conditions. The argument is not that any condition alone is sufficient; it's that all four together produce a structural posture that resists collapse.

## Open Notes

- The thread's argument is strongest for treaty-and-republic cases. It's weakest for the Hoshi case, where the "distribution" is across generations rather than across simultaneous offices. May need a sharper formulation in Section 3.3 that distinguishes intra-generational distribution (multi-office) from intergenerational distribution (across-time succession).
- The North Korea counter-example (planned for Chapter 6) is the hardest test. Need to be honest about it: does the framework predict North Korea's collapse incorrectly? If so, what does the framework get wrong about it? The honest answer is probably that the framework treats persistence-via-coercion as a fifth, undocumented condition that's distinct from the four institutional ones — a distinction the book may need to make explicit.
- The Acemoglu-Robinson "inclusive vs. extractive" framing is close enough to this thread that some readers will conflate them. Distinguish them clearly: their framing is about political-economic incentive structures; this thread's framing is about institutional persistence under stress. Related but not the same.
