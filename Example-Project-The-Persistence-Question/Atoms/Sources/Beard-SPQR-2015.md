# SPQR: A History of Ancient Rome

## Citation

Beard, Mary. *SPQR: A History of Ancient Rome*. London: Profile Books, 2015.

(Chicago Notes-Bibliography format; first footnote citation form; subsequent footnote citations use short-form *Beard, SPQR*, page.)

## Why it matters here

Beard's *SPQR* is the closest contemporary comparable to the book this manuscript is becoming. Trade non-fiction; structural-historical argument; sophisticated engagement with sources; readable across general audiences. It's also a substantive source for the book's Roman material (which appears across chapters but most centrally in Chapter 5's discussion of constitutional republics and Chapter 6's treatment of the Roman Republic as a counter-example).

Beard's specific arguments about the Republic's institutional structure (the mixed constitution, the cursus honorum, the role of *mos maiorum*) directly feed the book's treatment of distributed legitimacy at the Republic level.

Beyond content, *SPQR* is a model for the book's voice and posture: confident, willing to make claims, methodologically honest about evidence, accessible without dumbing down.

## Key claims / passages

- **Chapters 4–7** (the Republic chapters in Beard) — the mixed-constitution analysis. Beard's framing draws on Polybius but is more skeptical about how cleanly the mixed constitution worked in practice. Useful for the book's treatment of distributed legitimacy as both real and incomplete.
- **Chapter 1, pp. 17–35** — Beard's treatment of the founding legends (Romulus, Lucretia, etc.) as functional myths whose historical reliability is low but whose institutional function is high. Useful methodological model for the book's treatment of the Hoshi 718 AD founding date.
- **Chapter 8** — the Late Republic crisis. Beard treats the breakdown as structural, not as the fault of individual figures (Caesar, Sulla, etc.). The book's argument about the Roman Republic as a counter-example (in Chapter 6) follows Beard's structural framing.
- **Chapter 12** — Beard's account of Roman law as a written tradition that outlived the Western Empire. Direct support for the *Written Tradition* thread.
- **Epilogue** — Beard's brief reflection on why *SPQR* stops at 212 CE (Caracalla's universal citizenship grant). The book's epilogue may want to make a similar move on its own framework's limits.

## Worries

- *SPQR* covers 1,000 years in 600 pages. Beard makes choices about what to compress. The book this manuscript is becoming sometimes wants finer-grained Roman material than Beard provides; supplement with [[Cornell-Beginnings-of-Rome-1995]] and primary sources (Livy, Polybius, Tacitus) directly as needed.
- Beard's voice is excellent and very distinctive. The book this manuscript is becoming should not sound like *SPQR* — different argumentative shape, different scope. Voice contamination risk; flag during voice-pass.
- One specific point: Beard's epigraphic / archaeological framing is more skeptical of pre-Punic-War narrative material than some other Roman historians. The book may want to engage Cornell or Forsythe for a slightly less skeptical voice on Regal Period and early Republic material.
